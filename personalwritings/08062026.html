I Learned How to Go Offline By Being Online ; 08.06.2026

On my way home from class today, I bought a $2 puzzle from a thrift store.

This feels like an important detail to establish upfront: I don't particularly like puzzles. I don't have any fond childhood memories of completing them with my family on cold winter afternoons, nor am I the type of person who finishes one and frames it as wall art. To be quite frank, puzzles don't have the best track record in my life.

The last time I completed a puzzle was about five years ago, only because I'd contracted food poisoning from the undercooked chicken in my Chinese food takeout, and I did not have the energy to do anything other than sit at my dining room table, fighting through waves of nausea while I slowly pieced together David Bowie's face. From that point on, I could never stomach any Chinese food dish that includes chicken, and I was never quite fond of puzzles either.

But today as I strolled inside that thrift store, there it was: a little box containing hundreds of tiny pieces and, presumably, several hours of my future life.

I ponied up a Toonie because I thought I should be doing puzzles — not because I really wanted to, or had some positive memories attached to them.

I bought it because puzzles are something you do when you're trying to spend less time online.

And lately I've been trying to spend less time online.

Which means, apparently, I now own a puzzle.

There's an interesting trend I've noticed within the online communities centered around being offline, and that is, whether we like it or not, we're still being influenced by people online. Even though I'm making a dedicated effort of removing useless noise, I still find that some portion of my thoughts are not fully mine. Yes, I can say with confidence that I was the one who decided to go against the grain: deleting social media, making my laptop more work- focused, switching my iPhone out for a flip phone — all things which have made my life less convenient, and yet all the more satisfying — but how much of the process of arriving at those decisions was actually my own?

I've gotten so used to the comfort of having a device in my pocket that, at any moment, could make decisions for me; what kind of fall sweater I should buy based on the latest trends, or which book I should read based on the highest Goodreads score.

Technology doesn't only influence what we do; it also provides us with templates for what we should want, and, in turn, who we should become: the person who reads 50 books a year; the person who runs at 6 a.m.; the person who wears cozy grandma slippers and drinks coffee from a ceramic mug; the person who has deleted Instagram and spends Sunday afternoons hanging out with friends and doing puzzles.

I think, deep down, I wanted to be that last person.

I've started to wonder whether a lot of the online culture surrounding being offline is becoming yet another thing to consume. I know that, for me, it certainly has been.

I may not clock in two hours of mindless scrolling on Twitter or Instagram per day anymore, but I still check in on r/digitalminimalism and r/dumbphones for 40 minutes. I may not sit through endless video essays anymore (most of which I barely remember anyway), yet I still browse Pinterest for the best aesthetic images of analog activities, spending most of my evenings curating a vision board of how I'd like to spend my offline time.

Even though I may have changed what I'm consuming, it doesn't change the fact that I'm still consuming. I wasn't purchasing a puzzle because I really itched to do a puzzle. I wanted a quick way to obtain patience, focus, leisure, and overall, a life that wasn't being lived through a screen. I was hoping that this purchase would push me towards a new identity, the promise of being someone else.

I don't think there's anything wrong with puzzles. I don't think there's anything wrong with journaling, reading physical books, knitting, baking bread, taking walks, listening to music on mp3 players or CDs, or leaving your phone at home. I, in fact, do a large chunk of these things, and I do them with pride.

Maybe the problem is that we're always going to be influenced. The internet surely didn't invent influence; we have always learned to live life from other people. We copy our parents, our friends, our classmates, the people we admire. That' s just part of being human. But the internet has turned that natural process into something much more constant, a relentless whisper in your ear: "here's the kind of person you could be, if only you did X." And I guess in my case, the "person I could be" is someone who is focused, has his priorities straight, is sociable and intelligent, and has hobbies that live beyond a screen… and X = buy that puzzle.

I just don't want to accidentally replace my curated online life with a new form of offline curation.

Maybe the most ironic part about this whole thing is that I learned how to be offline from the internet.

I learned about dumbphones online. I found communities dedicated to digital minimalism online. I searched for analog hobbies online. I built a vision of my ideal offline life while sitting in front of a screen, anxiously waiting for that day when some fantastical force would magically propel me away from the desk in my bedroom and out into the real world.

At some point, I started wondering whether I'd actually escaped the influence of the internet or simply changed the aesthetic of what I was consuming.

I don't want an algorithm tempting me to scroll for three hours, but I also don't want a different algorithm telling me that the correct substitute to scrolling for three hours is to buy a linen-bound journal, read Tolstoy, and become someone who does puzzles.

Is there a version of me that exists beyond influencing? I don't know, and I doubt it. There is no Crow untouched by marketing, algorithms, friends, culture, books, movies, parents, or the internet. And maybe that's okay. Maybe that's not the goal. Maybe the goal is simply to occasionally stop and ask: Do I actually want this? Or do I just like the idea of being someone who wants this?

I want to figure out what I actually like without someone online telling me what I should like.

And I guess it may just have to start with completing that damn puzzle.
